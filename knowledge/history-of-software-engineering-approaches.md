# History of software engineering approaches

The lecturer's short history (from the textbook's chapter 2 and an assigned paper):

- **Early on**, around the world wars, computers and programming were new and each bloc (Japan in Asia, Britain and the US, continental Europe, Germany, Russia) programmed in its own way. Each thought its approach superior. Britain gained a reputation after wartime code-breaking at Bletchley Park (he recommended the film *The Imitation Game*).
- **After the Second World War**, countries spied on each other's methods and realised they were building in silos. They moved to formalise the field and share best practice; the terms **computer science** and later **software engineering** were coined in the 20th century.
- **Borrowing from engineering.** Traditional engineering was standard and tested, so people applied its rigour to software. "Software engineering" means applying that rigour. If you apply the ideas strictly, it is engineering; if you apply them arbitrarily, it is not.
- **Plan-driven** methods (waterfall) came first and were very strict. Then **adaptive/agile** methods arrived for flexibility. Today most companies use **hybrid** methods that mix both.

His warning about hybrid and agile methods: they have parts that are strictly guarded and parts left to interpretation. "It's agile, do whatever you want" is wrong. Don't tamper with the strict parts.

## Use it in the capstone

In the paper's method section, name our approach precisely (Scrum, or a hybrid) and show we followed its non-negotiable parts: roles, events, artifacts, timeboxes.
