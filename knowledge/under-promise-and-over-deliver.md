# Under-promise and over-deliver

The lecturer returned to this advice in two lectures.

**Lecture 8, product engineering.** A shop's bread often stays good for a day or a week past its label. Software can work the same way. A client may ask for ten features, and your research shows they will need two more. Deliver those too. When they find you did more than expected, they trust you and want to work with you again. Don't limit yourself to the exact edges they define. Promise less than you can achieve, so actual performance beats the promise.

**Lecture 9, timelines.** Even with AI speeding up development, he still plans team time from long experience. If a set-up task might take one or two days, he books five in the contract. Finishing early delights the client. Finishing late makes them call you useless. Clients don't care that you hit new challenges; they worry about competitors shipping first. So:

- Always give yourself enough room to deliver.
- But not too much. The product owner and client will push back on long estimates, and padding hurts your credibility.
- Pair this with honesty. If only five of eight items fit, say so up front (see [product backlog vs sprint backlog](product-backlog-vs-sprint-backlog.md)).

The two ideas fit together: be modest in what you commit to, generous in what you deliver, and never miss the commitment.

## Use it in the capstone

Plan our sprints with some buffer before the 18 Dec showcase. Track any extra features we add beyond the stated requirements, and present them as added value in the talk.
