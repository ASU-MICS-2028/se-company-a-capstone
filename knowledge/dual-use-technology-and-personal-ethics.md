# Dual-use technology and personal ethics

**Dual-use technology** can serve both peaceful and harmful purposes. Drones are a clear case: an agricultural spraying drone can be adapted for war with a few changes.

A student asked the visiting robotics engineer whether he would build drones if the defence ministry contracted him for warfare. His position:

- He **refuses to build military or warfare drones** because of his personal beliefs, even though he has the skills.
- He **will build capable general drones**. If a buyer later misuses one, he sees that as the buyer's responsibility. He builds to solve problems for people.

This is one view, and it is open to debate. Critics would say a builder who can foresee misuse still shares some responsibility. That is exactly the kind of question professional codes address.

The lecturer said the course's final week covers the **ACM Code of Ethics** (the Association for Computing Machinery's professional code for computing professionals). He said this case is something to consider when they get there. The code asks professionals to contribute to human well-being, avoid harm, and think about foreseeable uses of their work.

Questions to ask about any intelligent system:

- Who could misuse it, and how?
- Which design choices make misuse harder (access control, logging, limits)?
- Where do we draw our own line, and do we say so publicly?

## Use it in the capstone

The ACM paper should have an ethics section. Discuss foreseeable misuse of our AI component and the safeguards we chose, citing the ACM Code of Ethics.
