# Good governance checks power before a crisis

**Corporate governance** is the set of rules, roles and processes that decide who controls a company and how they are held to account.

Lecture 6 was a case seminar on Uber's founder-CEO, who was forced out in 2017. Under his leadership the company grew to about US$70 billion and around 14,000 staff. Meanwhile harassment complaints went unanswered, and the board had little power to challenge him. Eventually major investors confronted him outside a board meeting and demanded his resignation.

The main lesson from the session facilitator: **a governance framework must be able to check a powerful leader *before* a crisis, not only after one.** Put the limits in place early, so that when someone wants to overreach, the rule already stops them.

His reasoning:

- Being aggressive and driven can help a founder grow a company. But humans make mistakes, and we tend to romanticise aggressive leaders. Governance "protects you from yourself" and from others.
- Even an all-powerful CEO answers to the board. The facilitator described board meetings where CEOs feared by their staff were grilled hard.
- A leader who wants a big company will eventually need outside money, and investors will inspect the governance.

Without a framework, the only way to remove a problem leader is a power play (see [legitimate reason versus legitimate process](legitimate-reason-versus-legitimate-process.md)).

## Use it in the capstone

Nyansapo Systems should write down its own decision rules: who approves scope changes, merges and releases, and how disputes are escalated. Put them in the Scrum records so they exist before any conflict.
