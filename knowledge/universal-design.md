# Universal design

**Universal design** aims for products, environments and services that everyone can use, whatever their physical or cognitive abilities. In practice: a feature built for one group helps everyone. **Human-centred design (HCD)** is related but often narrower: it may target one specific user group (Harte et al. 2017).

The lecturer stressed this after two teams presented cafeteria designs, one for visually impaired students and one for students with ADHD. He linked it to the [double diamond](double-diamond.md): separate teams can **diverge** on different user groups, but must then **converge**, because their work depends on each other, as parallel agile teams do. In the prosits, each team names **one universal design opportunity** that helps both groups and probably everyone.

Examples that converge:
- **Save and resume.** Built for ADHD users who get interrupted, but nobody wants to lose a half-finished order when an app crashes.
- **Constant progress feedback** ("step 2 of 4", order status) and a short, predictable checkout.
- **Lower cognitive load.** Few categories, few decisions. "Keep it simple."
- **Status as explicit text,** not colour alone.
- **User-controlled notifications** in both sound and text. Audio built for blind users helps anyone whose eyes are busy.

The principle: you build for one user, and in practice you are building for one giant actor, everyone.

Inclusive design rule: identify concrete *interaction barriers*. Avoid stereotypes about a disability group.

## Use it in the capstone

In the UX doc, mark which accessibility features are universal and explain the benefit to all users. In the paper's design and ethics discussion (rubric F), name at least one such feature and say who it was tested with.
