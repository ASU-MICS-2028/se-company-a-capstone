# Timeboxing

**Timeboxing** means giving an activity a fixed time limit and keeping it. The lecturer called it very important.

- If the daily scrum is 15 minutes, it is 15 minutes; if 30, then 30. He discourages anything over **one hour**; long stand-ups become boring and bureaucratic.
- The **Scrum Master** runs the clock: an agenda, about two minutes per person, "time - next person".
- If the team keeps finishing early (say, three days in a row in 30 minutes instead of 60), agree to shorten the timebox.
- Sprints are timeboxed too, typically one or two weeks. If you can't finish, show what you have at the review, be accountable, push what works as an increment, and move the rest to the next sprint.
- Allowed, but not professional if it happens repeatedly. Prevent it by doing thorough research in sprint planning so the plan fits the time.
- Reviews and retrospectives can use the same regular meeting slot, maybe with a longer box (for example, one hour).

He noted that a previous cohort struggled with timeboxing from the first week to the last - meaning they never inspected and learned. Their sprint reviews were unplanned and chaotic. He asked teams to hold strict weekly meetings (in person, virtual or hybrid), each with a set task.

## Use it in the capstone

Record the planned and actual duration of each meeting in our Scrum records. Rehearse the conference talk to its time limit.
