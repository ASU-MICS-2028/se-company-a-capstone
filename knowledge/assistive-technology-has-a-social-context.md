# Assistive technology has a social context

The "logical" fix is not always the one people will use. The lecturer told of a young woman with partial hearing who had been abused in her community because of it. Given a hearing aid at a new school, she refused to wear it: people treated her differently when they saw it. She preferred to struggle, with a friend quietly standing by her good ear and relaying what was said.

Lessons:
- **Don't be robotic.** "There's a hearing aid, so she's fine" ignores stigma and how society perceives devices.
- Human interactions are subjective; design must consider how a solution makes the person look and feel.
- Offer graceful, discreet options so people are not singled out, such as fully visual or app-based engagement.
- Similarly, a separate priority queue helps, but be aware of how separation can feel.

## Use it in the capstone

In the ethics part of the paper and the UX doc, discuss stigma and dignity for our users, not just technical access, and prefer discreet interventions.
