# Walk the user's path

A **user journey** is the sequence of steps a person goes through to get something done. The lecturer's repeated instruction: don't assume the journey, **walk it**, physically, in the place where it happens.

- Put yourself in their place: "I have ADHD, it's lunchtime, I know the cafeteria is busy and I don't want to go. How do I get my food?"
- Go step by step (arrive, read the menu, pay, wait, collect) and ask at each step: what do they see first? Is it fine for them? Can we improve it?
- Don't rush. An AI tool gives you what others have already done online; walking the path gives you what your user actually needs.
- Aim for the reaction "I never even thought of that". Users who feel that become customers for life.

**Example.** A student with ADHD entering the cafeteria for the first time has to work out what to do, and the logic is not intuitive: most people go to whoever is closest to the food, but here you pay first and then collect. His fix: **large, colourful, numbered signs on the walls** (step 1 over the menu, step 2 over payment, step 3 at collection).

What this shows:

- Software is only part of the experience. Space, signs, queues and staff are part of the system, and some fixes need no IT at all.
- Reimagining the physical layout (self-order kiosks, status screens, a live menu display, speakers for ready orders) can be a valid part of a design proposal.
- Research counted as stronger evidence when it combined walking the space with talking to cafeteria staff and the campus accessibility centre, plus published articles on design for cognitive disabilities. Cite these in the report.

## Use it in the capstone

Do at least one site visit in our problem setting. In the UX doc, walk each persona through the journey step by step, noting the pain point and fix at each step (including non-software fixes), and cite the walk-through in the paper's method section.
