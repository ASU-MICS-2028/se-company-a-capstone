# Estimate effort before assigning work

The class asked: do you assign a task first, or estimate it first? The lecturer's answer from practice:

1. **First, a quick high-level split by skill.** "This part needs AI, so it goes to our AI lead. These screens go to UI/UX." People know their strengths, so this takes minutes.
2. **Then a proper estimate, led by the most experienced person in that area.** Only after that do you decide how many people the task needs. If the AI lead says "I need two people for a day," you add a helper.
3. **Then negotiation.** The product owner speaks for the client and pushes for speed: "Five days? Can we do four?" The tech lead pushes back if quality would suffer. The usual result is a compromise, such as four and a half days with lighter documentation. If they reach deadlock, the product owner calls the client, who usually accepts the team's number. Then the team must hit it, or finish earlier.
4. **The Scrum Master makes sure the estimation follows good practice.** Every item gets points, and the estimates are fair.

Why the order matters: if you hand a task to one person before estimating it, they may find it is too big. Then you have to go back and add people in the middle of the sprint.

The tech lead must know who can do what, and how fast. If one person is still working days after everyone else has finished, the estimate was wrong. That is a project-management failure, not that person's fault (see [dependencies and bottlenecks](dependencies-and-bottlenecks.md)).

## Use it in the capstone

Record who estimated each task and who owns it. With six people, name a lead for each area (AI, back end, front end, UX) so each estimate has an expert behind it.
